# Foundations — The Language of Grappling

Before getting into specific positions and techniques, there are a handful of principles that govern everything in jiu-jitsu. These are not warm-up concepts that you learn once and move past. They are the mechanics that make every technique in this reference work. A sweep works because of leverage. A pass works because of pressure. An escape works because of framing. If you understand *why* these principles matter, the techniques in the pages that follow will make far more sense — and you will be able to troubleshoot on your own when something is not working.

Think of this page as the vocabulary. Every page that follows speaks this language.

---

## 1.1 Positional Hierarchy

Jiu-jitsu has a positional hierarchy — a ranking of positions from most dominant to least dominant. Understanding this hierarchy tells you what you are trying to achieve at any given moment and what your opponent is trying to achieve.

From most dominant to least:

1. **[Back control](08-back-control.md)** — You are behind your opponent with your chest to their back and your hooks (legs) controlling their hips. This is the single most dominant position in jiu-jitsu. Your opponent cannot see you, cannot easily attack you, and you have access to chokes and controls that are extremely difficult to defend.

2. **[Mount](07-mount.md)** — You are sitting on top of your opponent's torso with your knees on either side of their body. You have gravity, weight, and leverage working in your favor. Your opponent's primary goal is to escape; yours is to attack or transition to the back.

3. **[Side control](06-side-control.md)** — You have passed your opponent's legs and are controlling them from the side, chest to chest. You are in a dominant position, but your opponent has more escape options than they would from mount.

4. **[Half guard (top)](04-half-guard-top.md)** — You are on top, but your opponent has one of your legs trapped between theirs. You are in a favorable position, but you still need to free your leg and complete the pass.

5. **[Inside the guard (top)](02-closed-guard.md#25-passing-closed-guard)** — You are between your opponent's legs. They are using their legs to control distance and attack. You need to pass their guard to improve your position.

6. **[Guard (bottom)](02-closed-guard.md)** — You are on your back, but your legs are between you and your opponent. This is not a bad position. The guard is where many attacks originate — sweeps, submissions, and transitions. A skilled guard player can be very dangerous from here.

7. **[Half guard (bottom)](03-half-guard-bottom.md)** — You are on the bottom with one of your opponent's legs trapped. This is a neutral position with options for both people. You can sweep, wrestle up, or attack submissions, but your opponent has passing options too.

8. **[Side control (bottom)](06-side-control.md#64-escaping-side-control-from-the-bottom)** — Your opponent has passed your guard and is controlling you from the side. Your priority is to escape — either by recovering your guard or by getting to your knees and wrestling up.

9. **Mount (bottom)** — You are underneath your opponent's mount. This is a bad position. Your only goal is to escape.

10. **Back control (bottom)** — Your opponent has your back. This is the worst position to be in. Escape is your only priority.

Turtle, the front headlock, and north-south sit outside this list — they are transitional positions with their own pages ([Turtle](10-turtle.md), [Front Headlock](09-front-headlock.md)).

The hierarchy is not rigid — a skilled guard player might be more dangerous from closed guard than an inexperienced grappler is from mount — but it provides the framework for decision-making. In general, you are trying to move up the hierarchy (improve your position), and your opponent is trying to do the same. When you have a dominant position, you have the option to either maintain it, improve it further, or attack a submission. When you are in an inferior position, your goal is to escape and recover to a neutral or dominant position.

This hierarchy also explains why position comes before submission. It is tempting, especially as a beginner, to grab whatever submission presents itself regardless of your position. Resist that temptation. Secure a dominant position first, then attack. A failed submission attempt from a bad position often makes your situation worse.

## 1.2 The Underhook

If there is one concept that appears more often in this reference than any other, it is the underhook.

An underhook means getting your arm underneath your opponent's arm, typically so that your hand ends up on their back or their hip. It sounds simple. It is the single most important positional battle in no-gi jiu-jitsu.

Here is why: whoever has the underhook controls the inside space. From half guard, the bottom player with the underhook can wrestle up to their knees and start attacking. The top player with the underhook can flatten their opponent and begin passing. From side control, the underhook allows you to control your opponent's far shoulder. From the clinch, the underhook gives you the angle to take the back.

**The underhook battle in half guard** ([bottom](03-half-guard-bottom.md#34-the-underhook-battle), [top](04-half-guard-top.md#the-underhook-battle-from-top)). This is where the underhook matters most, and where you will fight for it constantly. If you are on top of half guard with your right leg trapped, you are trying to get your right arm under your opponent's left arm and onto their back. Your opponent is trying to get their left arm under your right arm and down toward your hip. Whoever wins this battle has a significant advantage.

A few key details:

- **A bent underhook is a strong underhook.** The more your arm is bent and tight to your body, the more leverage you have. If your opponent straightens your underhooking arm — pushes it flat against your side — it becomes weak and they can start attacking it with kimuras or simply collapsing on it.

- **The underhook should stay low.** When you win the underhook from bottom half guard, you want your hand near their hip, not high on their shoulder. If you reach too high, your opponent can turn it into a whizzer (overhook) and neutralize it.

- **Use your legs to create the underhook, not just your arm.** This is a critical insight that many beginners miss. If your opponent has flattened you out in half guard and your arms cannot reach for the underhook, do not just reach with your arm — that exposes you to attacks. Instead, use an explosive movement with your legs: bridge, shrimp, or drive your knees into their body to push them up and back. As their posture breaks and space opens, *then* slide your arm through for the underhook. Your legs create the space; your arm fills it.

- **Arms and legs must work together.** This principle extends beyond the underhook. Throughout jiu-jitsu, your limbs need to coordinate. When escaping side control, you bridge first with your legs to create space, *then* use your arm frames to maintain that space while you hip escape. If you try to push your opponent off with just your arms while your hips stay flat on the mat, you will fail. Bridge first, then brace.

## 1.3 The Crossface

A crossface means driving your shoulder or forearm across your opponent's jaw, forcing them to look away from the direction they want to move. It is one of the most effective control tools in jiu-jitsu, and it works because of a simple biomechanical fact: your body follows your head. If you cannot turn your head to your right, you cannot shrimp to your right. If you cannot shrimp, you cannot escape.

The crossface appears everywhere in this reference:

- **From [side control](06-side-control.md#the-crossface) (top):** You reach your arm under their head, grip their far armpit with your middle finger, and pull them into your body while driving your shoulder down across their jaw. This turns their head away from you and prevents them from shrimping onto their side to escape. It is the foundation of maintaining side control.

- **From half guard (top):** Once you have the underhook and have flattened your opponent out, you establish head control by bringing your arm under their neck and clasping your hands. You drive your shoulder into their jaw to force them to look over their far shoulder. This makes it nearly impossible for them to bridge back toward you and recover.

- **From the [knee slice](04-half-guard-top.md#42-the-knee-slice):** As you pass through the knee shield and begin to consolidate side control, the crossface keeps your opponent from turning into you and recovering guard.

- **From [mount](07-mount.md#the-crossface-pressure):** You can use a crossface to make your opponent uncomfortable and force them to react — usually by turning to their side, which opens up attacks.

The key principle: wherever you are in a dominant position and your opponent needs to turn or shrimp to escape, the crossface prevents that turn. Learn to recognize when a crossface is available. It is often the difference between maintaining a dominant position and losing it.

## 1.4 Framing and Bracing

If the underhook is about winning the inside space and the crossface is about controlling your opponent's head, framing is about *creating* space when you are in a bad position. Frames are your primary survival tool.

A frame is a rigid structure you create with your arms — typically your forearm — braced against your opponent's body to keep their weight off of you and to create enough distance to move your hips. The two most common frames are:

- **Neck frame:** Your forearm braced against the front of their neck or under their chin. This keeps their upper body from collapsing onto you.
- **Hip frame:** Your forearm braced against their hip. This keeps their hips from pressing into yours and prevents them from advancing their position.

Together, these two frames create a pocket of space that allows you to hip escape (shrimp) and begin recovering your guard.

**Critical details about framing:**

- **Frame with your forearm, not your hand.** If you brace against their hip with an extended hand, they can drop their weight and injure your wrist. Use the bony part of your forearm. A good test: you should be able to wave your hand freely while framing. If your hand is doing the work, you are doing it wrong.

- **Bridge before you brace.** This is the most common mistake beginners make when escaping. They try to push their opponent off from a flat position on their back. This is a bench press, and your opponent's weight plus gravity will beat your arms every time. Instead: bridge first — drive your hips up explosively to lift their weight off you — and *then* extend your frames to keep them elevated while you move your hips out. Your legs create the space; your arms maintain it.

- **Double hip escape.** When escaping from side control, do not try to slide your knee in on the first shrimp. Instead, hip escape twice in a row. Bridge, brace, shrimp once to create initial space, then push off with your foot and shrimp again to create enough room to insert your knee. Trying to rush it in one motion usually results in getting stuffed.

- **When they sit to their hip, change your frame strategy.** If your opponent is in side control and they sit up to their hip (rather than staying chest-to-chest), your standard neck and hip frames become weak because the angles have changed. In this case, bring your far arm down to your ribcage to protect the underhook, and drop your near elbow to the mat as a block. Then shrimp away — their hips cannot follow you past your elbow. Recognize the difference between flat side control and seated side control, and adjust your frames accordingly.

## 1.5 Weight Distribution and Pressure

In jiu-jitsu, pressure is not about being heavy. It is about *where* you put your weight.

A 150-pound person who understands weight distribution will feel heavier on top than a 200-pound person who does not. The principle is straightforward: concentrate your weight through the smallest possible area of contact, and place it where your opponent is weakest.

A few examples of how this plays out:

- **In the [knee slice](04-half-guard-top.md#weight-distribution):** A common mistake is resting the full weight of your knee on your opponent's inner thigh, with your knee hovering above the mat. This creates a gap between your knee and the ground that your opponent can exploit to pull their leg through and recover guard. The correction is subtle but important: bring your knee through so it rests on the mat, with your upper shin pressing against their thigh. Now there is no gap. The pressure is lower and more distributed, but the position is airtight.

- **In side control:** If you are crossfacing your opponent and clamping down on their far side, it is easy to let your weight drift toward their far shoulder. This tips you off-balance — your head drops lower than your hips — and a good opponent can bridge and sweep you. The fix: even while pulling them into your body with the crossface, keep your hips sunk back toward your base. Your upper body controls them; your lower body keeps you stable.

- **In mount:** When you apply crossface pressure from mount to get your opponent to turn, you post out to one side and drop your weight. But you need to be deliberate about *where* that weight lands. If you commit too far, your opponent can pull your shoulder and sweep you. Keep the pressure uncomfortable but controlled.

The general principle: heavy hips, light hands. Your hips are your center of gravity and your primary tool for generating pressure. Your hands and arms are for controlling position, not for crushing. When in doubt, sink your hips.

## 1.6 Action and Reaction

Every technique in jiu-jitsu exists within a chain of actions and reactions. Nothing happens in isolation. When you attack one thing, your opponent defends — and that defense creates the opening for your next attack. This is perhaps the most important strategic concept in the art.

Some examples from later pages:

- You threaten a knee slice pass to one side. Your opponent raises a [knee shield](04-half-guard-top.md#43-defeating-the-knee-shield) to block. You elevate their knees as if to reset — and as they react to that, you either reslice on the same side or switch to a toreando pass. The first attack was not really the attack. It was the question. Their answer tells you what to do next.

- From closed guard, you attempt a [hip bump sweep](02-closed-guard.md#the-hip-bump-sweep). Your opponent posts their arm to stop the sweep. That posted arm is now vulnerable to a kimura. They defend the kimura by posturing up. Now their posture is broken and their neck is exposed for a guillotine. The sweep, the kimura, and the guillotine are all part of one chain.

- From mount, you apply crossface pressure to get your opponent to turn to their side. If they turn and reach, you feed their arm to a [gift wrap](07-mount.md#the-gift-wrap-to-back-control) and take the back. If they try to hip escape instead, you drop your hips and stuff the escape. You were not hoping for one specific outcome — you were creating a situation where any reaction your opponent makes gives you something to work with.

**Give to get.** A sharper version of the same idea: sometimes the fastest way to get what you want is to hand your opponent what *they* want, in a form you have already prepared for. Two examples, one from each side of the guard: passing an active butterfly guard by deliberately letting them have the underhook they are hunting for, then collapsing on it ([Open Guards](05-open-guards.md#giving-the-underhook)); and passing half guard by letting them rock you across their body and take the underhook, then dropping your hip onto it instead of fighting back to where you were ([Half Guard Top](04-half-guard-top.md#when-they-trap-your-leg-and-rock-you-across)). In both cases the opponent gets exactly the grip they wanted and it is worthless, because you knew it was coming.

This is why jiu-jitsu is often described as a chess match. You are not trying to force a single technique. You are presenting your opponent with dilemmas — situations where every response they have leads to a position that favors you. The techniques in this reference are presented in chains for this reason. Pay attention to the "if they do this, then you do that" branching. That is where the real learning happens.

## 1.7 The Two-on-One and the Arm Drag

The arm drag is a way of turning a grip on one arm into control of your opponent's whole body. It shows up standing, in [closed guard](02-closed-guard.md#the-arm-drag-to-back-control), in half guard, and in butterfly and open guard — it is a principle more than a technique.

**The two-on-one.** Start by taking one of their arms with both of yours. If their right hand is out, grip their right wrist with your left hand, then reach across with your right hand and grip the same wrist above your left. Two hands on one wrist is very hard to retract, and it lets you steer their posture.

**Break posture first, then drag.** Do not go straight from the two-on-one to the drag. Standing, pull straight down on the two-on-one so they bend over. In closed guard, pull them into you with your legs — the two-on-one alone cannot do it there, because they are already too close for you to extend their arm and generate pull. Once their posture is broken, slide your right hand behind their right tricep. Now drag: pull the tricep with your right hand and the wrist with your left so their arm crosses their own center line. That is the arm drag. Their back is now available.

**Head placement on the back.** Once you are behind them, keep pinning their dragged arm to their body and rest your head on that side — the side where the arm is trapped. On the other side, they can reach back and elbow you, which matters in a self-defense or MMA context.

**When the drag will not come across.** Against a strong opponent, the arm sometimes will not cross the center line. Rather than fighting for it, clamp: reach your other arm around their back to their far armpit, pull chest to chest, and wedge your body so the arm cannot retract. You have not dragged it, but it is just as trapped. The closed guard page has the full sequence that follows ([The Clamp Sweep](02-closed-guard.md#when-the-drag-will-not-come-across-the-clamp-sweep)).

The arm drag chain — back take, pendulum sweep, arm lock, and the clamp sweep — is drawn in [chains/09-arm-drag.md](../chains/09-arm-drag.md).

## 1.8 Mental Models for Rolling

Live rolling — sparring with a resisting partner — is where you test everything you have learned. It is also where beginners tend to get overwhelmed. A few mental frameworks can help.

**Use all of your tools.** It is easy to become fixated on whatever your opponent is attacking. If they are controlling your arms, your entire focus goes to your arms. But you have four limbs, and they work best in coordination. Think of your arms and legs as two separate units that can operate independently. While your opponent is focused on controlling your upper body, your legs may be free to create hooks, establish [butterfly guard](05-open-guards.md#51-butterfly-guard), or push off their hips. While they are focused on controlling your legs, your arms may be free to frame, pummel for underhooks, or push off their back. The most effective grapplers are the ones who keep *everything* moving with purpose.

**Know what position you are in.** This sounds obvious, but beginners often find themselves in positions they do not recognize. You cannot execute a technique if you do not know what position you are in. Before every roll and during every transition, ask yourself: where am I? Where are they? What is the positional hierarchy telling me to do right now — attack, maintain, or escape? Over time this becomes instinctive, but in the beginning it requires conscious effort.

**Have a plan, but hold it loosely.** Before a roll, it helps to decide on one or two things you want to work on: a specific sweep, a particular pass, a submission setup. This gives your rolling structure and helps you get repetitions on techniques you are learning. But be willing to abandon the plan when the situation changes. If you are so fixated on hitting your planned technique that you ignore the opportunities your opponent is giving you, you will miss the real learning.

**Position before submission.** As a beginner, this is the most important principle to internalize. When you find yourself in a dominant position, your first instinct will be to lunge for a submission. Resist that instinct. Secure the position first. Make sure you have a solid base, good control, and your opponent cannot easily escape. *Then* begin working toward a submission. A submission attempt from an unstable position usually results in losing the position entirely.

## Summary

These principles — positional hierarchy, the underhook, the crossface, framing, weight distribution, action-reaction, and the arm drag — will appear in every page that follows. When a technique is described, look for how it employs these principles. When a technique is not working for you in training, ask yourself which of these principles you might be violating.

The positions and techniques change. The principles do not.
